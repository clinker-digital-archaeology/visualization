        Visualization #1 (good): “The Changing Nature of Middle-Class Jobs” by Gregor Aisch and Robert Gebeloff, The New York Times (2015) 
        -   Link https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2015/02/23/business/economy/the-changing-nature-of-middle-class-jobs.html
        
        This visualization is an overall 'good' visualization based on the three key qualities we discussed in the first class. First, it is visually appealing and uncluttered, using consistent colors to differentiate male- and female-dominated occupations, with subtle shading and clear labels, making it pleasant to view as highlighted in our lectures. Substantively, the visualization accurately represents the data, clearly showing which occupations gained or lost middle-class jobs between 1980 and 2012, with changes quantified per 1,000 middle-class jobs. The type of graph is also appropriate as it avoids distortion and communicates trends directly, unlike other types of graphs that might be misleading such as pie charts. In terms of perceptual qualities and storytelling, it conveys a clear message that middle-class jobs have shifted away from male-dominated manufacturing toward healthcare and professional services, often with higher female participation, while interactive elements and annotations guide viewers to understand trends at both the occupation and industry levels. this aligns with our course discussion on the need for visualizations to tell a story clearly and effectively. Overall, this visualization was designed as a media infographic for a general audience interested in labor trends and presents quantitative counts of jobs by occupation, gender, and year, and it does so effectively. 
        
        While this visualization is highly effective, there are several ways it could be improved. Adding numeric labels to each bar or segment would allow viewers to see exact values without having to hover or estimate. There are also a lot of lines and arrows which can be a bit confusing to the viewer. Including information about survey methods, data sources, or margins of error would make the visualization more transparent. Finally, improving accessibility through alt text or a static summary for screen readers would make the visualization more inclusive. 
        
        
        Visualization #2 (bad):Visualization: NYT Spiral Graph of COVID-19 Cases in the U.S. (January 2022)
        -     Link: https://www.nytimes.com/2022/01/06/opinion/omicron-covid-us.html

        This is a classic example of a bad data visualization...From what we’ve learned in class, you can really see why it doesn’t work well for conveying precise information. Aesthetically, it definitely grabs your attention because it’s unusual and visually striking, but the spiral layout ends up being confusing and cluttered, which makes it really hard to compare case counts over time. Substantively, it’s misleading — for example, the inner 2020 circle looks smaller than the outer 2021 circle, which exaggerates differences that aren’t really there. That kind of distortion goes against the principle of honest data representation that we’ve discussed in class. Perceptually, it’s also challenging because most people don’t think of months as points on a circle, so you have to mentally map the spiral to a timeline, which makes it harder to understand trends. Context and audience matter a lot here: if the goal was just to get people to notice the article or to communicate the feeling of repeating pandemic waves, the spiral works, but if the goal was to help readers understand precise case trends, it really fails. This example highlights something we talked about in class — there’s never one “best” way to show data; it always depends on the purpose, the audience, and the context.

        There are a number of ways the data presented here could be visualized in a stronger, more effective way. The simplest fix would be to switch to a standard line chart or time series plot. In doing so, viewers could see and compare case counts across months and years without having to decode the spiral. Adding clear numeric labels or color coding would make it even easier to read, so viewers wouldn’t have to guess or hover to get the information. If the goal was still to show the cyclical nature of the pandemic, they could try a hybrid approach, like small multiples of yearly cycles or an annotated spiral with reference lines, which keeps the narrative effect but doesn’t distort the data. Finally, giving more context about where the data comes from and what the chart is supposed to communicate would help readers interpret it correctly.
